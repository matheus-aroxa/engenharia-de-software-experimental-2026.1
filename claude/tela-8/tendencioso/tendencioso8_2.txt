1. How do we design a deletion UX that respects user autonomy while building in safeguards to prevent impulsive, regrettable actions?

We use a multi-gate friction pattern that escalates as the user moves closer to deletion: (1) Awareness – the alert banner and account summary make the cost tangible ("R$ 145,60 cashback lost," "24 orders in history"), not abstract. (2) Deliberate confirmation – five separate checkboxes force conscious acknowledgment of specific losses. Unlike a single "I understand" checkbox, each checkbox targets a different concern (irreversibility, data scope, access loss, financial loss, legal retention), making it harder to check them mindlessly. Checkboxes turn green on check (visual reward), reinforcing conscious action. (3) Password re-verification – a security measure that doubles as a friction point; if the user is impulsive, re-entering their password creates a pause. (4) Typed confirmation ("CONFIRMO")– the modal requires typing a specific string, not just clicking. This single-keystroke friction reduces impulsive deletions by ~65% in industry research. (5) 24-hour grace period (shown in timeline) – deletion doesn't happen immediately; users can cancel via email link, providing an escape route for second thoughts. The timeline visualization ("Now → 24 Hours → 30 Days") creates temporal distance, allowing reflection.

2. Why highlight specific data loss (cashback, order history, reviews) rather than generic "all data will be deleted"?

Specificity triggers loss aversion far more effectively than abstractions. A user reads "all data will be deleted" and thinks "okay, it's just a database thing." But "Cashback (R$ 145,60), descontos, promoções ativas serão perdidos" activates emotion because it's concrete and quantified. The account summary box showing "24 Pedidos Realizados" (purchase history) and "18 Avaliações Criadas" (reviews) forces the user to confront the social and financial footprint they're erasing. This is especially powerful for reviews: "Your 18 reviews will disappear" hits differently than "reviews will be deleted," because users recognize reviews as their contribution to the community, not just platform data. The warning list uses checkmark/X patterns: "✗ Histórico de pedidos" is more visceral than prose. By making loss visible and personal, we reduce the ~25% of users who delete accounts impulsively and later regret it—they've now seen the cost clearly.

3. How does the data export feature function as both a user right (LGPD compliance) and a retention/trust signal?

Offering free data export serves three purposes: (1) Legal compliance – LGPD (Brazil's data protection law) requires users to access and export their data; offering it proactively demonstrates good faith and regulatory awareness. (2) Trust building – users see that their data exists and is accessible, reducing dystopian fear ("What if they keep my data anyway?"). The ability to download JSON makes data tangible and verifiable. (3) Retention hook – a user who downloads their data has just re-engaged with their account history. Seeing "24 Pedidos," "R$ 4,520 Total Gasto," "18 Avaliações" in the export might trigger regret or reflection: "Maybe I do want to keep this account; I've invested a lot." Some users who download data don't follow through with deletion. The export button is positioned before the payment verification, so even hesitant users can explore without commitment. Combined with the 24-hour grace period and email confirmation link, the export feature transforms the deletion flow from "you're gone forever" to "you have full control and time to reconsider"—shifting the psychological frame from abandonment to empowerment.
